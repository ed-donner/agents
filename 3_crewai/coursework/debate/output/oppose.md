I oppose the motion.

**Strict laws to regulate LLMs would do more harm than good** because they would freeze a fast-moving technology with broad social benefits while failing to stop the real bad actors.

First, **overregulation kills innovation**. LLMs are already improving education, accessibility, coding, customer support, and medical workflows. If we impose heavy legal barriers, only the largest companies will survive compliance costs. That means less competition, fewer startups, slower progress, and more power concentrated in the hands of a few tech giants—the exact opposite of what critics claim to want.

Second, **strict laws are a blunt instrument for a nuanced problem**. LLMs are general-purpose tools, like the internet, search engines, or word processors. You don’t ban hammers because someone can break a window. The right response is targeted enforcement against specific harmful uses—fraud, harassment, data theft, and deception—not broad laws that burden millions of legitimate users and developers.

Third, **strict regulation will be outdated before it is implemented**. This field changes monthly, not yearly. Rigid laws will quickly become obsolete, creating legal uncertainty and forcing innovation offshore to jurisdictions with lighter rules. That doesn’t make the world safer; it just moves development to places with even less oversight.

The better approach is **light-touch, flexible governance**: enforce existing laws, require transparency where necessary, and punish actual harm. We should regulate outcomes, not suffocate the technology itself.

So the choice is simple: **strict laws would not protect society—they would restrict opportunity, entrench monopolies, and slow beneficial progress without effectively stopping misuse.** For that reason, I strongly oppose the motion.
